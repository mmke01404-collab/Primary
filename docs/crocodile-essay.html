Field Notes on Preservation · No. 7

The Crocodile, the Ceramic Disc,
and the Very Confused Archivist

On the difference between surviving and continuing

I — The ContestantLet's talk about tough

People think they know tough. But none of them — not one — is as tough as a crocodile.

This old fellow's been knocking about for two hundred million years. Dinosaurs come and go. Continents rearrange themselves. Ice ages arrive uninvited. And there he is, floating down the river, thinking: lovely day.

Specimen A Living lineage — no case required

Then along comes an archivist who looks at this magnificent beast and says: "That's exactly like a ceramic storage disc."

Now, bear with me.

II — The UnderstudyAn expensive paperweight with excellent self-esteem

The disc is marvellous. Heat? Doesn't care. Radiation? Shrugs. Moisture? "Is that all you've got?" Its bits sit there for centuries without moving an inch. Splendid.

Except — there's one tiny problem.

The crocodile is alive. The disc is a very expensive paperweight with excellent self-esteem.

Specimen B Inert medium — awaiting a reader

He has children. Those children have children. Generation after generation they pass on what it means to be a crocodile. No instruction manual. No PDF. No README file. No poor engineer in the year 3126 desperately searching through ancient ruins asking, "Does anyone remember what this machine was supposed to do?"

The crocodile simply carries on being a crocodile. Meanwhile the ceramic disc is lying in a vault somewhere looking absolutely magnificent. Perfect bits. Perfect structure. Not the faintest idea what any of it means.

It's rather like discovering the complete works of Shakespeare engraved onto indestructible glass — except nobody remembers English. Or books. Or computers.

So the whole thing begins to unravel. Because surviving isn't the same as continuing.

III — What Survives Isn't the PointLineage

The bits surviving isn't the interesting bit.

Identity survives because somebody remembers where it came from, what changed, who changed it, why they changed it, and whether it's still the same thing.

That's lineage. Without lineage you've preserved something. Could be the Magna Carta. Could be somebody's holiday photos from Skegness. The disc certainly isn't telling.

IV — Evidence vs. HistoryThe wedding ring

A brand-new wedding ring sitting in a velvet box is flawless. Not a scratch. Beautiful. But it isn't the marriage.

Specimen C Left: evidence  ·  Right: history

The ring that's been worn every day for fifty years — polished smooth, covered in tiny dents and scratches — now that's carrying a life. One is evidence. The other is history. That's the difference.

And suddenly our immaculate ceramic disc starts looking less like an immortal crocodile and more like a museum exhibit proudly announcing: "Contents preserved forever. Meaning not included."

V — The VerdictThe strongest safe, and no key

Nobody's saying the ceramic disc isn't brilliant. It is. It's the fossil. It's the reference point. It's the wonderfully stubborn thing that refuses to decay.

But if you actually want something to remain itself for a thousand years, you need more than perfect storage. You need lineage. You need context. You need people making decisions, recording changes, and passing understanding from one generation to the next.

Otherwise you've built the strongest safe in human history — and lost the key.

Specimen D Storage without stewardship

VI — Back to the RiverJust another Tuesday

Which brings us back to the crocodile. He's still floating there. Completely unbothered.

No metadata. No XML. No semantic ontology. No preservation strategy. Just another perfectly ordinary Tuesday, being exactly what crocodiles have always been.

And somehow, that's the cleverest archival strategy of the lot.
