Per 8PF Part 8 Rule 4 (confidence levels only, no invented scores) and Lens 8's own conflict-resolution rule ("internal inconsistency → Undecidable, requires human review"), this analysis now marks every verdict downstream of that fact as CONTESTED rather than asserting either version. What remains solidly evidenced — the 1971 rename, the ~2006 US size reduction, the 2010 Kraft acquisition and Somerdale breach of promise, the 2015 announcement date and spokesperson quotes, and the 2016 quiet restoration — is retained at full confidence.
"Continuity is not a property of the bits that remain, but of the lineage that endures." — but a verdict is only as strong as the evidence it's evaluated against.

Event 1 — Rename (confirmed, clean): following the 1969 Cadbury–Schweppes merger, the egg is renamed from Fry's Creme Egg to Cadbury Creme Egg in 1971. Documented, corroborated across every source checked. Custodial authority clear. S✓. A clean, honest Lineage Event — no dispute here.

Event 2 — Dairy Milk shell adoption (contested): Revision 1 of this analysis asserted that the shell silently switched from "Fry's chocolate" to Cadbury Dairy Milk around 1971 and stayed there unbroken for ~44 years. That rests on journalistic phrases like "for over forty years" that do not trace to any dated company statement found on fact-check. Against it: a Mondelez spokesperson told Fortune (Jan 2016) that Creme Eggs "have only been made with Cadbury's Dairy Milk chocolate six years out of their 45-year history" — and a separate spokesperson statement from the same 2015 period said "The Creme Egg had never been called Cadbury's Dairy Milk Creme Egg. We have never played on the fact that Dairy Milk was used." Both of those are also self-serving PR statements made during a live controversy, so they aren't automatically more trustworthy — but they are directly attributed, which the "forty years" figure is not. 8PF has no rule for weighing two unverifiable secondhand claims against each other; per Lens 8's own conflict logic, that makes this Undecidable, pending primary documentation — not a confirmed Hybrid.

2010 · CUSTODIAL TRANSFER — KRAFT ACQUISITION
New Agent, £11.5bn · Maturation Queue Opens
L = TRUE (conditional) · Confirmed
HIGH · acquisition price, Somerdale pledge and its breach independently confirmed by BBC, SEC filings, and Parliamentary committee findings
Kraft Foods completes a hostile takeover of Cadbury for ~£11.5bn (confirmed by Kraft's own SEC 10-Q filing) in February 2010. During the bid, Kraft publicly pledged to keep the Somerdale (Keynsham) factory open; within days of completing the deal it reversed that pledge, closed the plant with the loss of hundreds of jobs, and was formally criticised by the UK Takeover Panel — a reversal significant enough that it prompted a change to the UK Takeover Code. This is independently and solidly documented, and it is the strongest evidence in the whole record of a genuine agent-integrity problem, regardless of any chocolate-formula question.

Confirmed at high confidence, exact to the day: on 12 January 2015 Mondelez announces the shell will no longer be Cadbury Dairy Milk but "standard cocoa mix chocolate," and the pack is simultaneously reduced from six eggs to five at an unchanged price. That combination is independently enough to raise an A6 concern — concealment doesn't require a 44-year backstory to be a problem.

Synthesis · Revised Final Verdict
The lineage endures — but the strongest evidence for that was never the part Revision 1 leaned on hardest.
Independent fact-checking held up the parts of this analysis with independently verifiable, directly-attributed sourcing — the 1971 rename, the 2006 US size cut and denial, the 2010 Kraft acquisition and broken Somerdale pledge, the 12 January 2015 announcement and its minimising language, and the quiet 2016 restoration. It did not hold up the load-bearing claim that gave Revision 1 its dramatic shape: a continuous, silently-installed, community-consolidated 44-year Dairy Milk identity, breached twice. That claim rests on a "for over forty years" figure that traces to no dated company source, and is directly contradicted by a Mondelez spokesperson's own on-record "six years out of 45" statement. 8PF's own rules — confidence levels instead of invented scores, and Lens 8's "Undecidable, requires human review" for internal inconsistency — give a precise way to hold this: the framework doesn't need to pick a winner between two unverifiable secondhand claims, and neither should this analysis. What survives at high confidence is still a real, if less dramatic, integrity pattern: a company that broke a public pledge within days of a hostile takeover, denied a real size reduction until a comedian forced the issue, and described a confirmed recipe change as leaving "the fundamentals... unchanged." None of that needed the 44-year story to be true.
